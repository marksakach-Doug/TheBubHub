Paradigm Shift

Seed cards are gone, and every card placed on the 4×4 grid must be something that actually happened to someone at the table. That means your four outer edges (North↔South and West↔East) have to do the heavy lifting: they must make players remember real moments instead of inventing plausible stories.

Why Writing Pole Pairs Feel Hard (And the Trick to It)

When you try to write two axes by hand, almost everyone falls into one of two traps. Once you see both traps, writing a pair takes three minutes:

Trap 1: The Diagonal Mirror (1 Axis Disguised as 2) Suppose you write North→South as "Guarded → Open" and West→East as "Hiding → Honest". Those feel like two axes, but they are both just "Closed → Open" twice!
NW: Guarded + Hiding (works) NE: Guarded + Honest (???) SW: Open + Hiding (???) SE: Open + Honest (works)
Look at North-East and South-West: they contradict themselves. Nobody at the table has a real memory that lives in NE or SW, so half your 4×4 board goes dead.
Trap 2: Chapter Titles Instead of Postures Words like "Vulnerability", "Boundaries", "Alignment", or "Closure" are essay topics. Nobody wakes up on a Tuesday and says, "I did a boundary today."
ABSTRACT: "Healthy Boundaries" (players invent speeches) CONCRETE: "Saying no without a 3-sentence excuse" (players remember a real text message)
The Trick: Cross Axis 1 (How You Carry It Inside) with Axis 2 (Where It Happens / How It Shows on the Outside).

Step 4 — The 4-Corner Cross-Breeze Test

Before you bring a pole pair to the table, test the four corner squares of your 4×4 board (`A1`, `A4`, `D1`, `D4`). If your two axes are genuinely perpendicular, each corner will summon a distinct, real memory. If `NE (A4)` or `SW (D1)` feels impossible or identical to another corner, your two axes are secretly the same axis!
